10 Healthy Freezer Lunches
Salmon, red lentil, quinoa and vegetable bowls
Designed to maximise vegetables and legumes, use mostly wholegrain/high-fibre carbohydrate, include fish/plant proteins and healthy fats, 
and minimise unnecessary added salt. This is an optimisation, not a claim that one recipe is universally 'the healthiest' for every person.
THE FINAL BATCH
Use all the lentils, salmon, eggs and fresh vegetables; use quinoa as the grain; keep seeds/tahini moderate; use 
only a little condensed soup; rinse the corn; add no salt unless the finished batch genuinely needs it.

 Psyllium husk - the batch is already very fibre-rich; extra psyllium is not needed and can make the texture gluey.
 Olives - optional in a normal diet, but leaving them out keeps sodium lower when the salmon, corn and condensed

Method
1. Rinse the quinoa thoroughly. Put 200 g quinoa in a saucepan with 450-500 mL water. Bring to the boil, cover, 
reduce to a very gentle simmer for about 12-15 minutes, then turn off the heat and rest covered for 5 minutes.
2. Rinse the 429 g red lentils. Simmer in about 1.3 L water for roughly 10-15 minutes, until tender but not completely 
collapsed. Drain any excess water.
3. Cook the vegetables with water rather than added oil. Start the 612 g carrots with a splash of water for about 7-10 
minutes. Add broccolini for about 3-4 minutes, then bok choy and spring onion for the final 2-3 minutes. Aim for just 
tender, because the meals will be reheated later.
4. Whisk the 7 eggs and scramble gently until fully set. Break into small pieces so they distribute evenly through all 10 
lunches.
5. Make the sauce: whisk together 100 g condensed tomato soup, 70 g tahini, 25 g garlic, 20 g ginger, cumin, 
turmeric, mixed herbs, chilli and plenty of black pepper. Thin with about 300-500 mL hot water until it is pourable.
6. In your largest clean pot or bowl, combine the cooked lentils, quinoa, vegetables, both drained/rinsed cans of corn, 
scrambled eggs and drained salmon. Flake the salmon through gently.
7. Stir in 150 g hemp seeds and 50 g flaxseed meal. Stir through 40 g nutritional yeast near the end. Add the sauce 
and enough extra water to make the mixture moist but not soupy.
8. Taste before adding any salt. The healthiest default here is no added salt; if the finished batch truly tastes flat, add 
only the smallest amount needed.
9. Weigh the finished batch (subtract the bowl/pot weight), divide by 10, and portion that exact weight into 10 shallow 
freezer containers. This gives genuinely equal meals rather than relying on sight.
Why these proportions are the health-optimised choice
Decision Reason
Vegetables + legumes first All 1,099 g of fresh vegetables are used, plus both cans of corn and all the 
lentils. Australian guidance emphasises plenty of vegetables and legumes; 
a standard vegetable serve is about 75 g.[1][2]
One grain, not three Quinoa is retained as the grain component, while white basmati rice and 
pasta are saved for another meal. The Australian Dietary Guidelines 
recommend grain foods that are mostly wholegrain and/or high in cereal 
fibre.[1]
Strong protein mix Salmon, lentils, eggs and seeds give a mix of fish, plant protein and egg. 
The Heart Foundation particularly favours legumes, nuts/seeds, fish and 
seafood as heart-healthy protein choices.[3]
Healthy fats without 
overdoing energy density
Salmon, hemp, flax and tahini provide unsaturated fats. The Heart 
Foundation recommends choosing unsaturated fats from foods such as 
nuts, seeds, olives and oily fish.[4]
Sodium kept down No added salt, rinsed corn, no olives and only 100 g condensed soup 
reduce avoidable sodium. FSANZ advises comparing sodium per 100 g on 
labels and choosing lower-sodium options where possible.[5]
No need to force every 
ingredient in
A healthier meal pattern is about proportions and food quality, not using the 
greatest number of ingredients. Adding all the rice, pasta, seeds and 
psyllium would make the batch much larger and more energy/starch dense

Cooling, freezing and reheating safely
 Portion the hot food into the 10 shallow containers rather than leaving it as one deep pot. Smaller, shallow portions 
cool faster.[6]
 Do not leave the batch sitting on the bench for hours. FSANZ's two-stage cooling benchmark for potentially 
hazardous cooked food is 60 C to 21 C within 2 hours, then 21 C to 5 C within the next 4 hours.[6]
 Refrigerate the portions promptly as they cool, with enough space around containers for cold air to circulate. Once 
properly chilled, freeze the meals you are not eating within the next few days.[6][7]
 Thaw in the fridge or microwave, not on the bench.[7]
 Reheat quickly until piping/steaming hot throughout. FSANZ advises reheating leftovers thoroughly; 75 C is a useful 
target when checking with a food thermometer.[7][8]
 Avoid repeated cool-reheat cycles: thaw/reheat the portion you intend to eat and reheat it once.[6]
