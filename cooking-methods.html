A MORE USEFUL QUESTION

There is no universal healthiest cooking method.

The right choice depends on food safety, vitamin retention, carotenoid availability, added oil, dietary pattern and how much of the food you actually eat. We compare real methods without inventing longevity scores, nutrient-retention percentages or exact optimum temperatures unsupported by studies.

01

Protect safety first

Poultry ≥75°C centre per FSANZ, fully set eggs for vulnerable people, cooked dried kidney beans and quickly chilled leftovers.

Keep water-soluble vitamins

Brief steaming or microwaving often limits losses compared with prolonged boiling of vegetables and discarding water.

Some heat unlocks nutrients

Heating tomatoes with a modest amount of olive oil can improve lycopene absorption; raw isn't always nutritionally superior.

Avoid unneeded charring

For starchy foods, lighter browning can reduce acrylamide exposure. Deep-frying usually adds more oil.

How we weigh evidence

Safety / regulatory guidance

FSANZ, NSW Food Authority and FDA guidance takes precedence over vitamins if a method leaves food unsafe. Temperature and timing must actually be measured where relevant.

Measured food composition

Cooking-method studies can compare vitamin retention, glucosinolates and fatty acids. Results often vary with produce variety, slice size, pan conditions and whether values are per raw weight, cooked weight or true retention.

Human nutrient absorption

Some small human trials show changes to lycopene or beta-carotene in blood. Those are surrogate measurements, not proof that eating those foods cooked that way makes people live longer.

Health outcomes and adherence

Long-term human trials that isolate boiling vs steaming vs frying for each individual food and measure mortality are generally unavailable. Overall food quality and safe, enjoyable habits carry more weight.

Coverage audits enumerate ingredient labels from all 17 currently active adult recipes plus the 35-line original V6 retailer basket. Multiple label descriptions are mapped to one food family.
